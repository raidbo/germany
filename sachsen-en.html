Learn More About Saxony

Saxony is located in eastern Germany and is particularly known for its important cultural cities, long industrial history and diverse landscapes. From the Leipzig Lowlands in the north to the Ore Mountains, Vogtland and Saxon Switzerland in the south, the federal state combines historic cities with impressive natural areas.

Further Information About Saxony

Geography and Landscape

Saxony is located in eastern Germany and borders Brandenburg and Saxony-Anhalt to the north, Thuringia to the west and Bavaria to the south. To the east and southeast, the federal state shares borders with Poland and the Czech Republic. The landscape changes from flatter areas in the north through hilly regions to the low mountain ranges in the south. Saxony is particularly shaped by the Ore Mountains, Vogtland, the Lusatian Highlands and Saxon Switzerland. In the southeast lies Saxon Switzerland, known for its striking sandstone rock formations and deeply carved valleys. The Elbe crosses Saxony from southeast to northwest and connects important landscape and settlement areas. The highest mountain in the Free State of Saxony is Fichtelberg in the Ore Mountains, with an elevation of 1,215 meters.

History of Saxony

The history of Saxony goes back many centuries and is closely connected with the Margraviate of Meissen, the later Electorate of Saxony and the Kingdom of Saxony. Under the House of Wettin, Saxony developed over the centuries into an important political and cultural center in Central Europe. In 1806, Saxony was elevated to a kingdom. Dresden developed into an important center of art, architecture and court culture. At the same time, significant mining and industrial areas developed, particularly in the Ore Mountains and other parts of the region. After the Second World War, Saxony became part of the Soviet occupation zone and later the German Democratic Republic (GDR). In 1952, the state was dissolved and its territory was divided among several administrative districts. Following German reunification, the Free State of Saxony was re-established in 1990.

Population and Cities

With around four million inhabitants, Saxony is one of the more populous eastern German states. A large share of the population lives in the three major cities of Dresden, Leipzig and Chemnitz and their surrounding areas. In addition, the state has numerous medium-sized towns, historic smaller cities and rural communities. Dresden is the state capital and an important cultural and political center. Leipzig is particularly known for its trade fair, university, musical tradition and growing importance as an economic and cultural city. Chemnitz has traditionally been shaped by mechanical engineering and industry and also plays an important cultural role. Other important cities include Zwickau, Görlitz, Plauen, Freiberg, Bautzen and Meissen. They differ considerably in their history, architecture and regional significance.

Economy

Saxony has a diverse industrial base and a long tradition in mechanical engineering. Today, particularly important sectors include the automotive industry, mechanical engineering, microelectronics, information and communication technology, as well as the pharmaceutical and healthcare industries. Leipzig, Dresden, Chemnitz and Zwickau are among the state's most important economic locations. Microelectronics plays a particularly important role in the Dresden region. The area is often referred to as “Silicon Saxony” and is one of Europe's leading locations for microelectronics and information technology. The automotive industry is also important to Saxony. Major vehicle manufacturing plants are located in Zwickau and Leipzig. In addition, numerous small and medium-sized enterprises contribute to the state's economy. Research and development also play an important role, particularly through cooperation between universities, research institutions and modern industrial companies.

Nature and Environment

Despite its industrial and urban regions, Saxony has a diverse natural environment. Large forested and low-mountain landscapes of the Ore Mountains and Vogtland are located in the south. In the east, the Lusatian Highlands and Upper Lusatia shape the landscape. Saxon Switzerland National Park is particularly well known for its unusual sandstone rock formations, gorges and viewpoints. The Elbe passes through the national park and, together with the distinctive rock formations, creates one of the most characteristic landscapes in Saxony. The Lusatian Heath and Pond Landscape is also important for nature conservation. It contains numerous forests, ponds and wetlands that provide valuable habitats for a variety of animal and plant species. Nature parks and protected areas also preserve other valuable landscapes throughout the Free State of Saxony.

Culture and Attractions

Saxony has an exceptionally diverse cultural heritage, particularly visible in Dresden, Leipzig, Chemnitz and the state's historic cities. Dresden is known for its important architectural landmarks and art collections. Major attractions include the Zwinger Palace, the Semperoper, the Frauenkirche and the Dresden Residence Palace. Leipzig has a strong musical tradition associated, among others, with Johann Sebastian Bach. The city is also known for its museums, St. Nicholas Church and its long history as a major trade fair center. In Meissen, the Albrechtsburg Castle and the cathedral are among the most important historic buildings. Görlitz is particularly notable for its exceptionally well-preserved historic architecture. Another outstanding destination is Saxon Switzerland, with the famous Bastei Bridge and its sandstone rock formations. The Ore Mountains also have a distinctive regional culture, including traditional handicrafts, wood carving and Christmas customs. The combination of music, architecture, art history, regional traditions and impressive landscapes makes Saxony a particularly diverse cultural and travel destination.
